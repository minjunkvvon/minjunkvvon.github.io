Military service · Commended

Co-designed a Quantum-Secured Frequency-Hopping System (QSFHS), a concept for hardening tactical radio communications against interception and jamming. The starting point was a weakness in how secure links are built today: classical encryption (RSA, ECC, AES) rests on mathematical problems that a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could unwind, and the pseudo-random generators (PRNGs) that drive frequency-hopping patterns are deterministic — given enough observation, a hop sequence can be predicted, and a predictable sequence can be jammed or listened in on.

The proposal replaces that deterministic core with physics. A quantum random number generator (QRNG) produces the hopping pattern from genuinely unpredictable quantum events — I looked at three physical sources: single-photon path choice at a beam splitter, photon arrival-time jitter, and vacuum-fluctuation noise, the last being the most compact to fabricate as a chip. The key exchange is then protected by quantum key distribution (QKD) using the BB84 protocol, where any eavesdropper unavoidably disturbs the photons' polarization states and is therefore detected the moment they try.

A design decision I argued for was not embedding a QRNG chip in every radio. Instead, a central QRNG generates the randomness and distributes synchronized hopping patterns and keys to endpoints over QKD. This keeps per-device cost and power draw low (military hardware has to be low-power and rugged), simplifies key management across a networked force, and improves security — a captured endpoint carries no generator of its own to compromise. I also surveyed transport options for the quantum channel (fiber, free-space optical, satellite-based, and emerging RF-band QKD) and their trade-offs in range, weather sensitivity, and cost.

Recognized with a commendation for contributing an idea applying quantum technology to secure military communications.
